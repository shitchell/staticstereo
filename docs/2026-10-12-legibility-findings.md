# Legibility and fusion

Three rounds of blinded trials, twelve reads total, one observer (Shaun). Every scene was
delivered as a URL with its text withheld; keys were generated by shuffle, written to disk,
and not read until after the reads were reported.

**The headline: the shipped legibility warning compares stroke width against the disparity
budget, and that is the wrong quantity.** Both halves of the rule are wrong — the threshold
is not budget-relative, and the budget governs something else entirely.

---

## 1. What the trials showed

### Round 1 — stroke ladder at constant weight (900), budget fixed at 18px

| size | stroke | read |
|---|---|---|
| 110px | 23px | ✅ exact |
| 90px | 19px | ✅ exact |
| 75px | **16px** | ✅ exact, *"fairly easy"* |
| 55px | **12px** | ⚠️ first word exact, second misread |

The 16px read **refutes the shipped rule outright**, which claims a feature narrower than
the 18px budget "will not fuse however long you stare."

The 12px failure is the most informative single datum in the set: `VELVET HARBOR` was read
as `VELVET MARBOR`. **Legibility degrades rather than failing** — one word survived — and it
degrades by *letter confusion*, not by blur. H and M differ by one stroke.

### Round 2 — budget varied, stroke held at 14px

Control first: changing `sepNear` leaves the measured stroke at exactly 14px, so the two
are independent and the comparison is clean.

| budget | read |
|---|---|
| 10px | ✅ exact |
| 18px | ✅ exact |
| 24px | ✅ exact |
| 30px | ✅ exact, *"had to try a bit harder to focus"* |

**4/4 across a 3× range of budget.** Legibility does not track the budget at all.

And the cell needing the most focus effort was the **largest** budget — the opposite of
what a budget-relative model predicts, and consistent with §2.3's finding that a wide
disparity range is harder to *fuse*. That is a different complaint from "I cannot resolve
the letters".

### Round 3 — size varied at constant stroke (14px): INCONCLUSIVE

| size | weight | stroke | read |
|---|---|---|---|
| 140px | 900 | 29px | ✅ *"easiest in a while"* |
| 140px | normal | 14px | ✅ *"not very hard, contrary to what i would have anticipated"* |
| 90px | bold | 14px | ✅ |
| 65px | 900 | 14px | ✅ |

**This round was designed badly and cannot answer its question.** Round 2 had already shown
4/4 reads at 14px, so building the discrimination test at 14px guaranteed a ceiling: with
every cell above threshold, size had no room to show an effect. "None of those was too bad"
is the correct reading of a test that could not fail.

Two things it did establish. Stroke matters monotonically — 29px was the easiest of any
trial. And **weight per se is not the operative variable**: normal weight at 140px was
expected to be hard and was not, consistent with stroke being what counts and weight merely
being the usual lever for reaching it.

### Fusion comfort — Shaun's own sweep, not a blinded trial

> "80 for sepNear was right at the edge of what's easy to focus on. 78 was the true limit.
> once i went below that, it became exceedingly difficult to keep my eyes focused on the
> text for more than a second or so at a time, then they'd unfocus, and i'd have to
> refocus again … 88-110 was pretty comfortable with no perceived increase/decrease in
> difficulty."

A **cliff at `sepNear` 78** (budget 32) and a **flat plateau from 88 to 110** (budget 0–22).
A four-point sweep could not have resolved that shape.

## 2. The two axes

The data separates cleanly into two independent effects, which the shipped rule conflates:

| axis | governed by | behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **can you resolve the letters** | stroke width, absolutely | threshold ≈ 12–14px, *independent of budget* |
| **can you hold fusion** | depth budget | flat to ~22px, cliff past ~32px |

They point in **opposite directions**, which is why conflating them produces actively bad
advice.

**Corrected 2026-10-15; this paragraph had the mechanism exactly backwards and three other
documents copied it.** It read: *"the current panel, seeing a thin stroke, effectively
suggests widening the budget — which does nothing for resolving letters and makes fusion
harder."* Measured against the shipped string, which says **"raise `sepNear` to *shrink*
the budget"**: shrinking moves the budget *toward* the plateau above, so fusion gets
**easier**, not harder. The advice is still worse than silence, for a different reason —
it **trades away depth range and buys nothing for legibility**, and the string advertises
the cost while the purchase does not exist. The conclusion survives; the sentence does
not, and the sentence is what got copied. Same shape as the `DEPTH_SOURCES` ruling: never
re-cite the sentence.

## 3. What drives the threshold — a better model than the one I proposed

I proposed that **minimum counter aperture** (the narrowest enclosed gap in a glyph) was the
quantity to measure, reasoning that a counter which closes up destroys the letterform.

**Shaun refuted it, and our own data agrees with him:**

> "an 'O' might have a small narrowest enclosed gap yet be fairly easy to distinguish since
> it is (1) a simple shape and (2) — perhaps more importantly — doesn't have a whole lot of
> similar letters to confuse it with … i think it is far more important if those smaller
> enclosed gaps obscure *distinct* parts of a letter that make it look more similar to other
> letters. that is: if you take the entire latin alphabet and change the stroke width, which
> sets of letters become visually more similar?"

The H→M error is exactly this. It was not a *visibility* failure — no feature was too small
to see — it was a **confusion** failure: the glyph collapsed onto a neighbour. Counter
aperture predicts O fails and H survives, which is backwards.

So the operative quantity is **discriminability within the alphabet**, not feature size.

**It is computable.** Render all glyphs at a given font, size and weight; take the pairwise
distance matrix over their masks; track the *minimum* pair distance as stroke thins.
Legibility should fail when some pair's distance crosses a threshold. This needs no theory
about which features a reader attends to, and it makes a falsifiable prediction: H/M should
appear as a near pair, and O should be far from everything.

Caveat Shaun raised and worth keeping: which features matter is partly person-to-person, so
a distance matrix is a proxy for a population, not for an individual.

## 4. Methodology lessons

Each of these cost a round, and all three are cheap to avoid.

1. **Warm-up is real and confounds every difficulty ranking.** *"the first time i watch any
   of these after a break, it's always a struggle. once i'm warmed up, it's easier."* Reads
   are robust (all-or-nothing, and they were correct); subjective difficulty orderings are
   not. Fix: an unscored warm-up stimulus, and each condition presented twice in randomised
   order so it appears both early and late.
2. **Sequential trials prime each other.** Round 1's `QUIET MARBLE` leaked into the next
   read as a guess of `MARBLE` for what was actually `HARBOR`. Fix: fully disjoint
   vocabulary across a set, no shared words and no near-rhymes.
3. **Do not design a discrimination test at a level already known to be above threshold.**
   Round 3's ceiling effect was entirely self-inflicted — Round 2's own result predicted it.

Also: deliberately unguessable word pairs beat famous phrases. A half-read of a known line
gets completed from memory, inflating apparent legibility.

## 5. What this means for the code

**`site/legibility.ts` is measuring the wrong thing.** Its threshold is
`sepFar - sepNear`, which Round 2 shows is irrelevant to legibility. Proposed replacement:

- compare stroke against an **absolute** threshold — 16px is the lowest value read *easily*,
  which errs toward warning;
- report the **budget separately** as a fusion-comfort note, with the cliff at ~32px (i.e.
  `sepNear` below ~78) as the thing actually worth warning about;
- stop implying that widening the budget helps legibility.

Not yet changed. The evidence is enough to show the current rule is wrong in direction; it
is one observer, one stroke level across budgets, and the warm-up confound is
unaddressed — so the replacement number deserves its own round rather than being swapped
in now.

## 6. Next steps, in priority order

1. **Rewrite the panel's rule** along §5. The current one gives advice that is worse than
   silence, so this does not need to wait for a more precise number.
2. **Re-run Round 3 near threshold (~12px stroke).** Matched cells exist: 900@55,
   bold@~78, normal@120 are all ≈12px across a 2.2× size range. This is the experiment that
   can come back negative, which Round 3 could not. It settles whether the panel needs one
   number or two.
3. **Build the alphabet distance matrix** (§3) and check whether it predicts the observed
   H→M confusion. If it does, it is a better metric than stroke width and can be computed
   offline per font.
4. **Speed.** Entirely untested, and suspected to interact with everything else. Round 3
   pinned speed at 170 px/s precisely so it could not contaminate the size comparison;
   varying it is its own round.
5. **Adopt the counterbalanced protocol** from §4 before trusting any further difficulty
   ranking.
